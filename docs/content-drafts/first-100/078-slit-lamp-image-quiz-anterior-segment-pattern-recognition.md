# Slit-Lamp Image Quiz: Anterior-Segment Pattern Recognition

This is a **text-first quiz framework**, not a set of slit-lamp photographs. It is designed to make the eventual photo quiz safer and more useful: read the location, layer, morphology, distribution and associated inflammation before you name a disease. That order is a good guard against the common exam error of recognising one memorable sign and ignoring the rest of the stem.

No clinical images, patient photographs, image captions, licences or provenance records are included in this repository. Consequently, this draft must **not** be published or represented as an image/photo quiz until a final editor supplies cleared, clinically verified images with an asset record and an educational caption for every prompt. The text may support postgraduate examination revision; it is not patient-specific diagnosis or management advice.

## The answer sequence to use before revealing a label

For every future image, say the following aloud.

1. **Site and layer:** lids/lashes, conjunctiva, corneal epithelium, stroma, endothelium, anterior chamber, iris or lens.
2. **Morphology:** staining defect, infiltrate, deposit, precipitate, cell/flare, membrane, opacity or pupil-margin material.
3. **Distribution:** central/peripheral, diffuse/focal, superior/inferior, unilateral/bilateral and any pattern relative to the pupil or limbus.
4. **Associated sign:** conjunctival injection, anterior-chamber activity, corneal oedema, iris change, pressure clue or lens finding.
5. **Nearest differential and limit:** give the leading pattern, then name the finding that would separate it from the closest alternative. Do not turn one morphology into a complete real-world diagnosis.

That last step matters. A prompt can test a classic association while clinical assessment needs history, examination and, where indicated, further investigation. In a viva, neutral description earns its place before an eponym.

## Twelve future image prompts with answer logic

Use each row as a concealed-answer card. An image should be added only when it has been cleared, its provenance recorded, and the caption tells the learner exactly which feature to inspect.

| Prompt for the future caption | Leading exam pattern | What the learner should describe first | Nearest differential / discriminator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Branching fluorescein-staining epithelial lesion with terminal bulbs | HSV epithelial keratitis pattern | branching epithelial staining and terminal bulbs | A zoster-associated pseudodendrite is generally elevated and does not have the classic terminal bulbs |
| Raised branching lesion with little or no classic terminal-bulb pattern | Herpes zoster pseudodendrite pattern | elevated epithelial configuration and staining behaviour | Do not call every branch a HSV dendrite |
| Epithelial defect overlying a focal stromal infiltrate | Microbial keratitis pattern | site, size, epithelial defect, infiltrate depth and anterior-chamber response | A corneal infiltrate alone does not identify an organism |
| Ring-shaped stromal infiltrate with compatible exposure and pain history in the stem | Acanthamoeba keratitis in the differential | ring, layer, pain context and any radial keratoneuritis | A ring is a clue, not a pathognomonic sign |
| Fine keratic precipitates concentrated inferiorly | Anterior-uveitis pattern | posterior-corneal location and inferior distribution | State the chamber cells/flare if the stem provides them; do not infer cause from KPs alone |
| Large greasy-appearing keratic precipitates | Granulomatous-inflammation pattern | size, morphology and posterior-corneal position | The appearance does not itself establish the aetiology |
| Layered white cells in the inferior anterior chamber, with or without fibrin | Severe anterior-segment inflammation pattern | hypopyon, fibrin and corneal context | A hypopyon is a sign; distinguish sterile and infective contexts from the full stem |
| Flaky material at the pupillary margin and anterior lens surface | Pseudoexfoliation-syndrome pattern | pupillary margin, lens-capsule distribution and associated iris findings | Pigment dispersion is a different pattern; seek its own posterior-corneal and iris clues |
| Vertical spindle-shaped pigment on posterior cornea | Krukenberg spindle | vertical posterior-corneal pigment distribution | Do not confuse pigment with keratic precipitates; morphology and context differ |
| Central posterior-cornea guttae | Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy pattern | endothelial/Descemet-level location and central distribution | Guttae do not label every cause of corneal oedema |
| Ring-like peripheral corneal opacity or deposit | Corneal degeneration/dystrophy differential | layer, distance from limbus and whether the lesion is raised or staining | Describe before using a dystrophy eponym; several patterns can be ring-like |
| Central posterior lens opacity | Posterior subcapsular cataract pattern | lens location relative to capsule and visual axis | Name the location, not just “cataract”; it separates this from nuclear and cortical patterns |

## How to make the same twelve prompts progressively harder

### Pass 1: identify the compartment

Cover the answer column and give only the site and layer. For example: “This is a posterior-corneal finding,” or “This is an epithelial staining pattern with an underlying stromal component.” If the layer is wrong, do not force a diagnosis. Review the relevant anatomy, then retry the same prompt.

### Pass 2: add morphology and distribution

On the next attempt, include two visible descriptors. “Inferior fine keratic precipitates” is more defensible than “uveitis”; “central guttae at the endothelium” is more informative than “corneal dystrophy.” The exercise is deliberately built around this distinction because an examiner can follow your observation even if they then ask for a different differential.

### Pass 3: give a differential boundary

Finish with one limitation: “The morphology supports an HSV epithelial pattern; I would distinguish it from a pseudodendrite by the terminal bulbs and the complete clinical context.” This makes the answer precise without pretending a photograph settles every diagnosis.

## The four high-yield contrast cards

| Contrast | Say this first | Trap to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Dendrite vs pseudodendrite | epithelial branching pattern, terminal bulbs and elevation | calling all branching lesions HSV |
| Infiltrate vs epithelial defect | stromal opacity/infiltrate versus surface staining defect | naming an organism from morphology alone |
| Keratic precipitates vs pigment | posterior-corneal distribution, size and shape | treating all posterior-corneal marks as inflammatory KPs |
| Guttae vs a stromal opacity | endothelial/Descemet-level versus stromal location | calling every central corneal change a dystrophy |

## Build an image-asset record before an editor adds a picture

Each proposed image needs all of the following, saved with the asset rather than reconstructed from memory later:

- a stable source or original-file identifier;
- written licence/permission status and permitted educational use;
- de-identification and consent status when applicable;
- a clinically verified diagnosis or deliberately limited morphology caption;
- the exact visual cue being assessed and a text alternative for learners who cannot see the image;
- alt text that describes the visible pattern without claiming more certainty than the image permits; and
- a reviewer name and review date.

If any one item is missing, retain the row as a text prompt. It is preferable to publish a clearly labelled text-first revision exercise later than a misleading “image quiz” with uncertain provenance or an overconfident caption.

## Caption templates an editor can use once assets are cleared

The caption should not simply repeat a diagnosis label. It should direct attention to one observable feature, tell the learner which part of the image is relevant, and state an uncertainty boundary where the photograph cannot settle the question. These templates can be adapted only after the asset record is complete.

| Pattern family | Caption structure | Accessible text alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Epithelial stain | “Inspect the fluorescein-staining epithelial pattern at ____. The feature being tested is ____.” | “Text prompt: branching epithelial stain with terminal bulbs; identify layer, morphology and closest differential.” |
| Stromal infiltrate | “Inspect the focal stromal opacity beneath/with ____. Describe its site, edge and associated surface defect before giving a pattern.” | “Text prompt: epithelial defect with a focal stromal infiltrate; state the descriptive grid before naming a pattern.” |
| Posterior-cornea finding | “Inspect the posterior corneal surface, especially ____. Is the material fine, large, pigmented or diffuse?” | “Text prompt: posterior-corneal finding with stated distribution; distinguish pigment, KPs and guttae by morphology.” |
| Anterior-chamber finding | “Inspect the inferior chamber and pupil area. Describe layering, fibrin or cells before suggesting an inflammatory pattern.” | “Text prompt: layered white cells/fibrin in the anterior chamber; explain why the sign alone does not establish cause.” |
| Iris/lens finding | “Inspect the pupillary margin and anterior lens surface. Name the anatomical location before the syndrome or cataract subtype.” | “Text prompt: flaky pupillary-margin/lens material or a central posterior lens opacity; localise before labelling.” |

An accessible alternative must be a real learning route, not the words “image unavailable.” It should give enough morphology for the reader to run the same five-step sequence, while making it clear that it is a simulated examination prompt rather than a clinical observation. Do not hide the answer in alt text when the image is meant to be assessed first; place the full descriptive prompt and answer in a nearby expandable or separately labelled quiz element.

## A short self-marking rubric

Give one point for each of these: correct compartment, accurate morphology, useful distribution, appropriate leading pattern, and a sensible differential boundary. A response that says only “HSV keratitis” might earn the pattern point but loses the observation points. Conversely, a candidate who says “a branching fluorescein-staining epithelial lesion with terminal bulbs, most consistent with an HSV epithelial pattern; I would distinguish a pseudodendrite by elevation and lack of classic bulbs” has demonstrated both recognition and restraint.

Use the rubric to target revision. Three consecutive `layer` errors call for anatomy review; repeated `differential boundary` errors call for contrast cards; recurring `distribution` errors need deliberate verbal practice with the caption. It is a revision framework, not a prediction of examination performance.
