This is a text-first production framework for a future labelled fundus-image quiz. It does **not** contain, supply or claim to contain 25 clinical photographs. A photograph may be added only after its rights, provenance, de-identification and caption have been cleared. Until then, use the 25 prompts below to rehearse the answer structure rather than to make a diagnosis from an imagined image.

For each future image, give three answers before revealing the caption:

1. **Pattern or diagnosis:** name the most defensible exam-level label.
2. **Visible cue:** point to the feature that made that label plausible.
3. **Nearest differential:** state one feature that would move you to the closest alternative.

That third line matters. A fundus photograph can show morphology; it does not provide the full history, symptoms, examination, OCT, angiography or clinical context. This is postgraduate exam revision for doctors, not patient-specific diagnosis or management instruction.

## How to use the visual quiz when the cleared images are attached

Cover the caption and take 20 seconds for the first pass. Start with distribution: disc, macula, posterior pole, arcade, sector or peripheral retina. Then decide whether the dominant finding is vascular, haemorrhagic, exudative, pigmentary, elevated retina or optic-disc change. Only after that should you attach a disease name.

On the second pass, say the contrast out loud. For example, “widespread haemorrhages in all quadrants suggest a central rather than branch venous distribution,” or “a full-thickness break supports a rhegmatogenous mechanism rather than traction alone.” A short contrast sentence is more useful than rereading a caption until it feels familiar.

Do not treat caption order as a clue. Randomise the 25 images at upload, preserve the answer key separately, and include an annotation only where it is needed to make the intended feature visible. An arrow can help a learner see a disc haemorrhage or peripheral break, but an arrow must not conceal uncertainty or make the caption claim more than the image supports.

## The 25-image content blueprint

The entries below are not image descriptions masquerading as images. They are editorial specifications for a licensed/cleared teaching-image set. “Answer” means the caption's intended exam-level answer, subject to the final editor confirming that the actual image visibly supports it.

### Vascular and diabetic-retinopathy patterns

| # | Intended caption answer | The visible feature the image must show | Closest contrast to include in the answer key |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Central retinal artery occlusion pattern | posterior-pole retinal whitening with a relatively red fovea | A cherry-red spot is a pattern clue; the full clinical context is not visible in a photo. |
| 2 | Central retinal vein occlusion pattern | widespread retinal haemorrhages with venous dilatation/tortuosity | Contrast a panretinal distribution with a sectoral branch-vein pattern. |
| 3 | Branch retinal vein occlusion pattern | haemorrhage and venous change confined to a venous sector | State the involved territory rather than calling every haemorrhagic fundus CRVO. |
| 4 | Diabetic macular oedema pattern | macular hard exudates, with context if oedema is being asserted | Hard exudate is not a direct measurement of retinal thickness; OCT is structural context. |
| 5 | Proliferative diabetic retinopathy | new vessels at the disc or elsewhere, clearly distinguished from intraretinal abnormalities | Neovascularisation defines PDR; IRMA is intraretinal. |
| 6 | Mild NPDR pattern | microaneurysms as the only visible DR lesion category | Do not substitute a dot haemorrhage for the classic microaneurysm answer. |
| 7 | Severe NPDR pattern | multiple severity features without visible neovascularisation | Caption the observed features; do not force a severity grade if the image lacks the necessary field. |

The International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy scale distinguishes microaneurysms, NPDR severity and proliferative disease by lesion pattern, with neovascularisation defining PDR.[^1] The image editor should avoid captions that make a complete severity classification from a crop that cannot show the required retinal distribution.

### Macular and retinal-detachment patterns

| # | Intended caption answer | The visible feature the image must show | Closest contrast to include in the answer key |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 8 | Non-neovascular AMD pattern | drusen with pigmentary change | Drusen are at the RPE–Bruch membrane interface; distinguish them from hard exudates. |
| 9 | Neovascular AMD pattern | macular haemorrhage/exudative appearance, captioned as a pattern | Fundus appearance alone does not define lesion subtype; cite imaging/context where needed. |
| 10 | Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment | elevated, often corrugated retina plus a visible causative full-thickness break if captioned as RRD | A break is the mechanism-defining feature. |
| 11 | Exudative retinal-detachment pattern | a detachment image without a break or tractional membrane visible | Avoid using shape alone as a diagnostic rule. |
| 12 | Tractional retinal detachment | fibrovascular traction elevating the retina | Identify the pulling membrane and distinguish it from a break-driven detachment. |
| 13 | Lattice degeneration | peripheral lattice change, ideally with an adjacent hole if that is the teaching point | Lattice is degeneration/risk context, not itself retinal detachment. |
| 14 | Retinal break with associated detachment | a peripheral full-thickness break and adjacent subretinal fluid | Caption the break location only when it is actually visible. |

For detachment questions, mechanism comes before appearance: a rhegmatogenous detachment requires a retinal break; tractional and exudative mechanisms do not.[^2][^3] The final caption should describe what is visible, then say which additional information would be needed if the photograph cannot establish the intended category.

### Pigmentary, inflammatory and optic-disc patterns

| # | Intended caption answer | The visible feature the image must show | Closest contrast to include in the answer key |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 15 | Healed chorioretinal-scar pattern | sharply demarcated chorioretinal scars with an appropriate description | Do not name aetiology from appearance alone without supporting history/context. |
| 16 | Retinitis pigmentosa pattern | bone-spicule pigment migration | Name the pigmentary pattern before inferring genotype or subtype. |
| 17 | Retinitis pigmentosa supporting signs | attenuated vessels and waxy disc pallor, if visible | These are supporting signs, not a replacement for the whole phenotype. |
| 18 | Optic-disc oedema / papillitis pattern | a hyperaemic swollen disc with blurred margins | A photograph cannot independently settle the cause of disc oedema. |
| 19 | Neuroretinitis pattern | disc oedema with a macular star | The caption should say “pattern” unless history and full assessment are part of the teaching case. |
| 20 | Glaucomatous optic-neuropathy pattern | enlarged cup with a rim finding that is plainly visible | Cup appearance is interpreted with disc size, fields and other context. |
| 21 | Optic-disc haemorrhage | splinter/flame haemorrhage at the disc rim | It can be glaucoma-associated but is not diagnostic of glaucoma by itself. |
| 22 | Optic atrophy pattern | disc pallor, ideally with a distribution such as temporal pallor | Pallor needs clinical and functional correlation. |
| 23 | Posterior-uveitis differential pattern | yellow-white chorioretinal lesions with recorded distribution | Describe distribution first; do not overclaim a specific aetiology from a single photograph. |
| 24 | Retinal vascular-tumour pattern | orange-red lesion and, if visible, feeding/draining vessels | Refer onward for a full differential rather than presenting one appearance as final diagnosis. |
| 25 | Macular-hole pattern | a macular lesion compatible with a hole, with OCT named as required staging context | Fundus alone is insufficient for full OCT staging. |

Retinitis pigmentosa teaching images commonly use bone-spicule pigmentation, vessel attenuation and disc pallor as pattern descriptors.[^4] A disc-oedema-plus-macular-star image can support a neuroretinitis pattern, but the visible pattern is not a patient-specific conclusion.[^5] Those boundaries should remain in every caption.

## Caption specification: the part that makes a quiz safe and useful

Each uploaded photograph needs a compact but complete caption. Use this order:

> **Answer:** [pattern/diagnosis at the supported level].  
> **Visible cue:** [specific feature and location].  
> **Nearest differential:** [one contrast and its discriminator].  
> **Boundary:** [history, examination or imaging needed before any fuller conclusion].

For example, a detachment slide should not say only “retinal detachment.” It should name the supported mechanism if a break or tractional membrane is visible, identify the cue, and state that a photograph cannot replace the complete assessment. A diabetic-retinopathy slide must likewise avoid labelling “severe NPDR” if a crop does not establish the full distribution required by the scale.

Image captions also need alt text that describes the visible educational feature without duplicating the answer key word-for-word. Alt text is not a substitute for a caption; it makes the item more accessible when an image cannot be seen.

## Asset gate: no image, no image quiz

This draft remains on publication hold. Before it is called “25 Photos” or uploaded as an image quiz, the editor must complete all of the following for **every** image:

- Record the original source, creator/owner, rights holder, licence or written permission, and the date that permission was checked.
- Confirm that the asset is genuinely cleared for this website and proposed commercial/editorial use; a search result, screenshot or unattributed social-media image is not provenance.
- Confirm de-identification and any consent/ethics requirements with the asset owner. Do not infer consent from an image being online.
- Check that the visible feature supports the precise caption; replace or downgrade a caption where the photograph is ambiguous, cropped or low quality.
- Store a clinically reviewed answer key, cue, differential and boundary statement with the image record.
- Write meaningful alt text and make any arrow, crop or annotation non-destructive and labelled.

Until that checklist is complete, publish this only as a text-first editorial plan or keep it unpublished. It is better to omit an image than to attach an unverified picture to a confident clinical label.
