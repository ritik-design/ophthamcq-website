The **vitreous humour**, also written *vitreous humor*, is a transparent, avascular extracellular gel behind the lens and in front of the retina. Its functions include light transmission, mechanical support, molecular diffusion and control of the vitreoretinal interface. For exams, its collagen-hyaluronan structure explains ageing, posterior vitreous detachment and traction.

> **Clinical scope:** This guide is for ophthalmology exam revision. It does not replace assessment of a patient with new flashes, floaters, reduced vision or field loss.

<!-- section_id: vh_key_takeaways -->
## Key takeaways

- The vitreous is 98 to 99.7% water, yet its behaviour depends on a sparse collagen and hyaluronan matrix.
- Type II collagen is the main fibrillar collagen. Hyaluronan keeps the fibrils apart and the gel hydrated.
- The vitreous base is the strongest attachment. Adhesions also occur at the optic disc, macula and retinal vessels.
- Age-related liquefaction alone does not produce a complete PVD. Vitreoretinal adhesion must also weaken.
- Persistent focal adhesion during separation can produce a retinal tear, vitreomacular traction or macular hole.

<!-- section_id: vh_composition -->
## What is the vitreous humour made of?

The vitreous is 98 to 99.7% water. Its gel structure comes from a three-dimensional network of heterotypic collagen fibrils, mainly type II collagen, separated by hydrated hyaluronan. Smaller components include other collagens, glycoproteins, metabolites and cortical hyalocytes. The small solid fraction controls most of the tissue's mechanical behaviour.

The useful mental model is a hydrated matrix, not a bag of water. Collagen supplies the framework. Hyaluronan occupies the spaces around it, binds water and resists compression. Fine, well-spaced fibrils limit light scatter. When they aggregate, the tissue becomes mechanically less uniform.

| Component | Main role | Exam note |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Optical medium and solvent | Almost all vitreous volume |
| Type II collagen | Main fibrillar scaffold | Predominant collagen |
| Type IX and V/XI collagen | Associated parts of heterotypic fibrils | Important, but not predominant |
| Hyaluronan | Hydration, spacing and compression resistance | A glycosaminoglycan |
| Hyalocytes | Matrix turnover and immune functions | Concentrated in the cortex |

A 2026 review by Ngo and colleagues describes the optical logic: collagen fibrils plus water bound to hyaluronan minimise light scattering while retaining stability. That relationship is a better viva answer than the water percentage alone.

## How is the vitreous body organised?

The vitreous occupies most of the posterior segment. A practical anatomical map divides it into central vitreous, a denser peripheral cortex and the vitreous base. Anteriorly it moulds around the posterior lens; posteriorly the cortex lies against the retinal internal limiting membrane. Cloquet canal crosses the central gel.

### Vitreous cortex

The cortex is the outer, collagen-rich zone. Its posterior part is closely apposed to the retinal internal limiting membrane. Hyalocytes are concentrated here. During vitrectomy, transparent posterior cortical vitreous can be hard to see, so residual adhesion matters when traction is being relieved.

### Vitreous base

The vitreous base straddles the ora serrata, with attachment to pars plana anteriorly and peripheral retina posteriorly. It is the strongest vitreoretinal attachment and generally does not release during an uncomplicated PVD.

### Cloquet canal and embryology

Cloquet canal follows the former course of the fetal hyaloid artery from the optic-disc region toward the posterior lens. It is a potential channel, not a functioning adult vessel. Pair it with:

- **Mittendorf dot:** a remnant at the posterior lens capsule.
- **Bergmeister papilla:** a glial remnant at the optic disc.
- **Persistent fetal vasculature:** persistence of fetal vascular and primary vitreous structures.

For more anatomy and embryology, use the [ICO Basic Visual Sciences resource](/products/ico-basic-visual-sciences).

<!-- section_id: vh_attachments -->
## Where does the vitreous attach to the retina?

The vitreous base is the strongest attachment. Additional adhesions occur around the optic disc, at the macula and along major retinal vessels. The posterior lens has a separate anterior vitreous attachment through the hyaloideocapsular region. The exam point is the hierarchy, because unequal release creates focal traction during vitreous separation.

Use this order:

1. **Vitreous base:** strongest and most persistent.
2. **Posterior lens region:** hyaloideocapsular attachment.
3. **Optic-disc margin:** release can leave a Weiss ring.
4. **Macula:** persistent focal adhesion can distort the fovea.
5. **Retinal vessels:** traction may contribute to a tear or haemorrhage.

A list alone is a weak viva answer. Add that pathology appears when the gel changes and these adhesions release at different rates.

<!-- section_id: vh_functions -->
## What are the functions of the vitreous humor?

Vitreous humor function can be grouped into four jobs: transmit light, support ocular shape, permit diffusion and regulate the mechanical relationship with the retina. Its matrix also affects oxygen distribution and cellular movement. Once the gel liquefies or separates unevenly, the same interface can become a source of retinal or macular traction.

### Optical and mechanical roles

The avascular gel gives light a clear path from lens to retina. Transparency depends on collagen fibril spacing within hydrated hyaluronan. The matrix also resists compression, supports globe contour and contributes to the relationship among lens, retina and posterior segment. It does not produce intraocular pressure.

### Transport and interface roles

Glucose, lactate, oxygen and other small molecules move through vitreous. The gel contains antioxidants, including ascorbate, and helps shape oxygen gradients. Normal vitreous also limits cellular movement. Diseased or residual cortex can instead provide a scaffold for proliferative tissue.

<!-- section_id: vh_ageing_pvd -->
## How does ageing lead to posterior vitreous detachment?

Ageing promotes collagen aggregation, gel liquefaction and weakening of post-basal vitreoretinal adhesion. Liquid vitreous can then pass through a defect in the posterior cortex into the retrohyaloid space. A complete PVD develops only when liquefaction and interface dehiscence occur together; either process alone can leave persistent traction.

Learn the mechanism in order:

1. Type IX collagen is progressively lost from collagen-fibril surfaces.
2. Collagen fibrils aggregate instead of remaining evenly spaced.
3. Liquid cavities enlarge. This is **synchysis**.
4. Remaining gel contracts or collapses. This is **syneresis**.
5. Liquefied vitreous crosses a defect in posterior cortex.
6. Posterior cortex separates where adhesion has weakened.

Le Goff and Bishop connect the final events: collagen aggregation favours liquefaction, while adhesion weakening permits separation. J. Sebag summarised the same requirement in *Ophthalmology*: "PVD results from gel liquefaction and concurrent dehiscence of vitreoretinal adhesion."

<!-- section_id: vh_retinal_tear -->
## Why can a PVD produce a retinal tear?

A retinal tear can occur when posterior cortex moves but a focal adhesion does not release. Traction is concentrated at that point, especially near predisposed peripheral retina or a vessel. If liquefied vitreous passes through the break, it can separate neurosensory retina from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium.

The causal chain is short enough for a viva:

**vitreous movement -> focal traction -> retinal break -> subretinal passage of liquid -> rhegmatogenous retinal detachment**

A Weiss ring supports release from the optic-disc margin. It does not prove that posterior cortex has detached everywhere. Symptoms also do not show whether peripheral retina is intact.

For a patient, sudden new flashes, a shower of floaters, reduced vision or a curtain-like field defect need prompt retinal assessment. This article cannot determine urgency for an individual eye.

<!-- section_id: vh_vma_vmt -->
## How do VMA and VMT differ on OCT?

Vitreomacular adhesion, or VMA, is persistent vitreous attachment at the macula without associated distortion of retinal anatomy. Vitreomacular traction, or VMT, adds a structural effect such as foveal contour change, intraretinal cystic change or elevation. The distinction is attachment alone versus attachment that deforms the macula.

| Finding | Posterior vitreous status | Macular effect |
|---|---|---|
| VMA | Perifoveal separation with residual macular attachment | No traction-related retinal distortion |
| VMT | Perifoveal separation with residual macular attachment | Foveal distortion or intraretinal change |
| Full-thickness macular hole | Often includes anteroposterior and tangential traction | Full-thickness foveal defect |
| Epiretinal membrane | Fibrocellular tissue on retinal surface | Surface wrinkling and variable distortion |

<!-- section_id: vh_surgery -->
## What matters during vitreoretinal surgery?

Vitrectomy removes gel to clear opacity, gain posterior access or relieve traction. Surgeons may need to induce a PVD, identify residual cortical vitreous and shave the peripheral gel while respecting the firm vitreous base. Gas, air and silicone oil provide tamponade, but none reproduces normal vitreous biology.

- Triamcinolone particles can outline transparent residual vitreous.
- Traction at the base can produce an iatrogenic retinal break.
- Gas and silicone oil are tamponade agents, not full biological replacements.

Common indications include selected retinal detachments, non-clearing vitreous haemorrhage, macular hole, epiretinal membrane and tractional disease. The exact indication and timing depend on the clinical setting.

## The 60-second viva answer

The vitreous is a transparent, avascular extracellular gel that fills most of the posterior segment. It is 98 to 99.7% water, but its structure depends on heterotypic collagen fibrils, predominantly type II, separated by hydrated hyaluronan. It has central, cortical and basal regions, with the strongest attachment at the vitreous base. Other adhesions occur at the disc, macula and retinal vessels. Ageing causes synchysis and syneresis. A PVD requires gel liquefaction plus weakening of vitreoretinal adhesion. Persistent focal adhesion can cause a retinal tear, VMT or macular hole.

<!-- section_id: vh_exam_traps -->
## Common exam traps

- Vitreous is **avascular and paucicellular**, not empty of cells.
- Type **II** is the predominant collagen type.
- The vitreous base is the strongest attachment.
- **Synchysis** means liquefaction; **syneresis** means contraction or collapse.
- A Weiss ring indicates peripapillary release, not separation of every posterior cortical segment.
- VMA has no traction-related distortion; VMT does.
- Cloquet canal follows the fetal hyaloid pathway, not adult central retinal circulation.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the main function of the vitreous humour?

The vitreous provides a transparent optical path and a hydrated mechanical matrix. It also permits molecular diffusion and influences adhesion between posterior cortex and retina. These interface mechanics explain why age-related separation can remain harmless in one eye but create focal traction in another.

### What is the vitreous humour made of?

It is 98 to 99.7% water with a sparse matrix of collagen and hyaluronan. Type II collagen is the predominant fibrillar collagen. Hyaluronan spaces the fibrils and retains water. Smaller components include other collagens, proteins, metabolites and cortical hyalocytes.

### What is the strongest vitreous attachment?

The vitreous base is the strongest attachment. It straddles the ora serrata and remains firmly connected to pars plana and peripheral retina. Optic-disc margin, macula and retinal vessels are additional sites of adhesion; posterior lens relates to anterior hyaloid through the hyaloideocapsular region.

### What is the difference between synchysis and syneresis?

Synchysis is liquefaction of vitreous gel, producing or enlarging liquid spaces. Syneresis is aggregation and collapse of the remaining gel. They often progress together with age, but the terms describe different physical changes and should not be exchanged in an examination answer.

### Why does PVD sometimes cause a retinal tear?

Posterior cortex may remain fixed at a focal adhesion while surrounding vitreous moves. This concentrates traction and can produce a retinal break. Liquefied vitreous may then enter subretinal space through that break, creating the mechanism for rhegmatogenous retinal detachment.

## Five practice MCQs

### 1. The predominant fibrillar collagen in adult vitreous is:

A. Type I  
B. Type II  
C. Type III  
D. Type IV

**Answer: B. Type II.**

### 2. The strongest vitreoretinal attachment is at the:

A. Fovea  
B. Optic-disc margin  
C. Vitreous base  
D. Major retinal vessels

**Answer: C. Vitreous base.**

### 3. Synchysis describes:

A. Gel contraction  
B. Vitreous liquefaction  
C. Retinal separation from RPE  
D. Macular surface fibrosis

**Answer: B. Vitreous liquefaction.**

### 4. Persistent macular attachment with foveal distortion is:

A. VMA  
B. VMT  
C. Complete PVD  
D. Retinoschisis

**Answer: B. VMT.**

### 5. Cloquet canal follows the former course of the:

A. Central retinal vein  
B. Hyaloid artery  
C. Long posterior ciliary artery  
D. Vortex vein

**Answer: B. Hyaloid artery.**
