The **layers of the eye** are organised into three concentric coats: an outer **fibrous coat** formed by the cornea and sclera, a middle **vascular coat or uvea** formed by the iris, ciliary body and choroid, and an inner **neural coat** formed by the retina. That three-line answer is sufficient for a basic anatomy question. A postgraduate ophthalmology answer must go further: identify where each coat changes character, which boundaries are potential spaces, how the structures are derived embryologically and why particular disease processes follow particular layers.

## Key Takeaways

- **Three coats:** fibrous outside, vascular in the middle and neural inside.
- **Transitions matter:** the limbus, ora serrata, Bruch membrane and optic nerve head are more examinable than a bare list of structures.
- **Dual retinal supply:** the central retinal circulation supports the inner retina; the choroid supports the outer retina.
- **Embryology crosses coats:** surface ectoderm, neural crest, neuroectoderm and mesoderm all contribute to the eye wall.
- **Clinical localisation:** layer-based anatomy explains retinal detachment, uveitis, scleral disease and OCT patterns.

## The three coats at a glance

| Coat | Components | Principal role | High-yield transition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outer fibrous coat | Cornea and sclera | Optical refraction anteriorly; protection and shape throughout | Corneoscleral junction at the limbus |
| Middle vascular coat (uvea) | Iris, ciliary body and choroid | Pupil control, accommodation, aqueous production, vascular and metabolic support | Iris root to ciliary body; ciliary body to choroid at ora serrata |
| Inner neural coat | Retina: neural retina and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) | Phototransduction, neural processing, metabolic and barrier functions | Photosensitive retina ends at ora serrata; non-visual retina continues over ciliary body and iris |

A useful mental map is **strength outside, circulation in the middle, signal processing inside**. It is an oversimplification, but it provides a reliable starting structure for viva recall.

## First coat: the fibrous tunic

The fibrous coat is a continuous outer shell. Its anterior transparent one-sixth is the cornea; its posterior opaque five-sixths is the sclera. Both contain collagen, but their organisation, hydration and cellular structure differ profoundly.

### Cornea: the transparent anterior component

The cornea contributes most of the eye's refractive power because of the air–tear interface and its curvature. Transparency depends on regular stromal collagen architecture, relative dehydration maintained by the epithelium and endothelial pump, avascularity and the absence of myelinated nerves within the optical zone.

The classical five corneal layers, anterior to posterior, are:

1. epithelium;
2. Bowman's layer;
3. stroma;
4. Descemet membrane;
5. endothelium.

Some contemporary descriptions include a pre-Descemet stromal layer, but examination answers should follow the terminology specified by the relevant syllabus or source. Do not casually convert a “five-layer” question into a debate about nomenclature.

The epithelium is non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium with basal cells, wing cells and superficial cells. Bowman's layer is an acellular, condensed anterior stromal layer that does not regenerate as a true anatomical layer after injury. The stroma accounts for about 90% of corneal thickness. Descemet membrane is the endothelial basement membrane and thickens with age. The endothelium is a non-replicating or very limited-replicating monolayer in vivo whose pump–leak function maintains deturgescence.

For cornea-specific clinical integration, continue to the [cornea topic guide](/topics/cornea).

### Sclera: the load-bearing posterior component

The sclera is dense, irregular connective tissue composed predominantly of type I collagen, with variable fibril diameter and arrangement. This irregularity scatters light and contributes to opacity—the reverse of the highly ordered corneal stroma.

From superficial to deep, the sclera is often described as:

- **episclera**, a vascular connective-tissue layer;
- **scleral stroma or substantia propria**, the main collagenous layer;
- **lamina fusca**, a thin, pigmented inner zone adjacent to the suprachoroidal space.

Its thickness varies. The sclera is thickest near the posterior pole and thinnest just posterior to the rectus muscle insertions, an important fact in surgical anatomy and a common MCQ.

Posteriorly, scleral collagen forms the **lamina cribrosa**, through which retinal ganglion-cell axons exit. The lamina is biomechanically important in glaucomatous optic neuropathy. Around the optic nerve, sclera becomes continuous with the dural sheath. Anteriorly, sclera merges with cornea at the limbus.

### The limbus is a transition zone, not a line on a diagram

The limbus is where transparent cornea meets opaque sclera and conjunctiva. Internally, the transition includes the trabecular meshwork and scleral spur; externally, it contains the palisades of Vogt and the limbal epithelial stem-cell niche. The limbus therefore links surface biology, aqueous outflow and surgical landmarks.

## Second coat: the vascular tunic or uvea

The uveal tract consists of the **iris, ciliary body and choroid**. It is pigmented and vascular, but its three regions perform different tasks.

### Iris

The iris is the most anterior part of the uvea and forms a contractile diaphragm in front of the lens. Its central aperture is the pupil. The anterior border layer and stroma contain melanocytes, vessels and nerves; the posterior surface has a heavily pigmented bilayered epithelium.

Two muscles control pupil size:

- **sphincter pupillae**: circular, parasympathetic, derived from neuroectoderm;
- **dilator pupillae**: radially arranged myoepithelial processes, sympathetic, also derived from neuroectoderm.

This is a favourite embryology trap: most smooth muscle is mesodermal, but the iris sphincter and dilator arise from optic-cup neuroectoderm.

The iris divides the space between cornea and lens into anterior and posterior chambers. Aqueous flows from the posterior chamber through the pupil into the anterior chamber. The iris root is its thinnest region and inserts into the anterior ciliary body.

### Ciliary body

The ciliary body extends from the iris root to the ora serrata and has two parts:

- **pars plicata**, the anterior folded portion containing ciliary processes;
- **pars plana**, the posterior flatter portion.

The ciliary muscle has longitudinal, radial and circular fibre groups. Contraction reduces zonular tension and permits the elastic lens to become more convex during accommodation. The ciliary processes produce aqueous humour, and the zonular fibres arise in relation to the ciliary epithelium and valleys between processes.

The ciliary body is covered internally by a double epithelium continuous with the optic cup: an outer pigmented layer and an inner non-pigmented layer. Their apical surfaces face each other. Tight junctions between non-pigmented epithelial cells contribute to the blood–aqueous barrier.

### Choroid

The choroid extends from the optic nerve to the ora serrata. It is a highly vascular, pigmented tissue between sclera and retina that supplies the outer retina, including the photoreceptors.

From outside to inside, remember:

1. suprachoroid;
2. large-vessel layer (Haller layer);
3. medium-vessel layer (Sattler layer);
4. choriocapillaris;
5. Bruch membrane at the choroid–RPE interface.

**Bruch membrane** is classically described as a five-layered extracellular matrix complex between the RPE basement membrane and the choriocapillaris basement membrane. Age-related change in this interface is central to drusen formation and age-related macular degeneration.

The suprachoroidal space is a potential space between choroid and sclera. Fluid, blood or inflammatory material can separate these layers. In contrast, the choroid is firmly attached at the optic nerve, vortex-vein exits and scleral spur.

## Third coat: the neural tunic

The retina is derived from the optic cup and has two major components:

- an outer **retinal pigment epithelium** derived from the outer layer of the optic cup;
- an inner **neural retina** derived from the inner layer of the optic cup.

The potential space between them reflects their embryological apposition. In retinal detachment, neurosensory retina separates from the RPE; it does not split away from choroid as a single combined sheet.

### The ten retinal layers

From vitreous to choroid, use the sequence:

1. internal limiting membrane;
2. nerve fibre layer;
3. ganglion-cell layer;
4. inner plexiform layer;
5. inner nuclear layer;
6. outer plexiform layer;
7. outer nuclear layer;
8. external limiting membrane;
9. photoreceptor inner and outer segments;
10. retinal pigment epithelium.

A concept is more durable than a mnemonic: **axons and ganglion cells are inner; photoreceptor nuclei and segments are outer; synapses lie in the plexiform layers**.

The internal limiting membrane is the retinal surface facing the vitreous and is formed largely by Müller-cell endfeet and basement-membrane components. The external limiting membrane is not a true membrane; it is a line of junctional complexes between Müller cells and photoreceptors.

### Retinal blood supply follows the layer

The inner retina is supplied principally by the central retinal circulation. The outer retina, especially the metabolically active photoreceptor outer segments, depends mainly on diffusion from the choriocapillaris. This dual supply helps explain why different vascular occlusions create different structural patterns on OCT.

### RPE functions worth stating in a viva

The RPE:

- absorbs stray light;
- forms the outer blood–retinal barrier through tight junctions;
- transports ions, water and metabolites;
- phagocytoses shed photoreceptor outer segments;
- participates in the visual cycle;
- secretes trophic and signalling factors;
- maintains the subretinal environment.

Calling the RPE merely a “pigmented layer” leaves most of the marks on the table.

### Ora serrata and the non-visual retina

The photosensitive neural retina ends anteriorly at the ora serrata. The two epithelial layers continue over the ciliary body and posterior iris as the non-visual part of the retina. This continuity is both anatomically elegant and embryologically testable.

## Embryology mapped to the three coats

The coats are anatomically concentric but do not arise from three single embryological sources.

| Structure | Principal embryological origin |
|---|---|
| Corneal epithelium | Surface ectoderm |
| Corneal stroma and endothelium | Neural crest |
| Sclera and choroidal stroma | Neural crest, with regional mesenchymal contributions |
| Lens | Surface ectoderm |
| RPE and neural retina | Neuroectoderm of optic cup |
| Ciliary and iris epithelium | Neuroectoderm of optic cup |
| Iris sphincter and dilator | Neuroectoderm |
| Vascular endothelium | Mesoderm |

The exam trap is obvious: a layer's adult position does not predict a single embryological origin. The outer coat alone contains derivatives of both surface ectoderm and neural crest.

## Layer-to-pathology correlations

### Fibrous coat

- Corneal ectasia reflects altered stromal biomechanics.
- Scleritis involves deeper scleral tissue and is clinically distinct from superficial episcleritis.
- Glaucomatous axonal injury is influenced by the optic nerve head and lamina cribrosa environment.

### Uveal coat

- Anterior uveitis primarily involves iris and anterior ciliary body.
- Choroiditis affects the posterior uvea and may secondarily disturb the outer retina and RPE.
- Suprachoroidal haemorrhage separates choroid from sclera within a potential space.

### Neural coat

- Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment separates neurosensory retina from RPE.
- Retinal vascular disease preferentially alters layers supplied by the affected vascular system.
- Macular disorders are localised by OCT according to the retinal layer involved.

This structure-to-disease mapping is the easiest way to convert an anatomy fact into a clinical MCQ answer.

## Common exam traps

- The three **coats** are not the same as the ten **retinal layers**.
- Cornea and sclera are continuous parts of the fibrous coat despite radically different transparency.
- The uvea is iris + ciliary body + choroid; it does not include retina.
- The external limiting membrane is a junctional line, not a true basement membrane.
- Bruch membrane lies between RPE and choriocapillaris, not between sclera and choroid.
- Retinal detachment is separation of neurosensory retina from RPE.
- Iris sphincter and dilator are neuroectodermal despite being muscles.
- The sclera is thinnest just behind the rectus insertions.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the three layers of the eye?

They are the outer fibrous coat (cornea and sclera), middle vascular coat or uvea (iris, ciliary body and choroid), and inner neural coat (retina, including neural retina and RPE).

### Which layer of the eye contains the retina?

The retina forms the inner neural coat. Its neural retina performs phototransduction and early visual processing, while the RPE supports photoreceptors and forms the outer blood–retinal barrier.

### Is the choroid part of the retina?

No. The choroid is the posterior portion of the middle vascular coat. It lies between sclera and RPE and supplies the outer retina through the choriocapillaris.

### Where does the cornea become sclera?

They meet at the corneoscleral limbus, a transition zone containing clinically important surface, angle and outflow structures.

### What separates the retina from the choroid?

The RPE lies against Bruch membrane, which interfaces with the choriocapillaris. In retinal detachment, the separation occurs between neural retina and RPE.

## Five practice MCQs

### 1. Which combination forms the fibrous coat of the eye?

A. Iris and ciliary body  
B. Choroid and retina  
C. Cornea and sclera  
D. Sclera and choroid

**Answer: C. Cornea and sclera.** They form the continuous outer coat, specialised for transparency anteriorly and mechanical support posteriorly.

### 2. The uveal tract consists of:

A. Cornea, iris and lens  
B. Iris, ciliary body and choroid  
C. Sclera, choroid and retina  
D. Ciliary body, retina and optic nerve

**Answer: B. Iris, ciliary body and choroid.** These are the anterior, intermediate and posterior components of the vascular tunic.

### 3. Bruch membrane lies between the:

A. Neural retina and vitreous  
B. RPE and choriocapillaris  
C. Choroid and scleral stroma  
D. Lens and anterior vitreous

**Answer: B. RPE and choriocapillaris.** It is an extracellular matrix complex at the outer retina–choroid interface.

### 4. Which ocular muscles are derived from neuroectoderm?

A. Superior and inferior recti  
B. Ciliary muscle only  
C. Iris sphincter and dilator  
D. Orbicularis and levator

**Answer: C. Iris sphincter and dilator.** Both arise from optic-cup neuroectoderm, an important exception to the usual mesodermal origin of muscle.

### 5. The sclera is thinnest:

A. At the posterior pole  
B. At the limbus  
C. Just posterior to the rectus insertions  
D. Around the optic nerve

**Answer: C. Just posterior to the rectus insertions.** This is a high-yield surgical anatomy fact.
